*Emerging Infectious Diseases*

We submit "Public Surveillance Benchmark for Reported Hantavirus Incidence,
EU/EEA, 2019–2023" for consideration as a Research article in *Emerging
Infectious Diseases*.

This manuscript reports a reproducible public-surveillance benchmark for reported
hantavirus incidence in EU/EEA countries, 2019–2023. Its public-health
contribution is not a deployable prediction system, but a calibration-aware
evaluation showing that sparse annual public data do not support strong covariate-
driven forecasting claims and that simple surveillance-history baselines remain
difficult to beat. This result is timely because public attention to rare
zoonoses — including the May 2026 WHO-reported Andes virus cruise ship cluster —
can create pressure for overconfident interpretation of limited surveillance data.
Transparent benchmarks that explicitly quantify the limits of sparse public
reporting are a practical tool for calibrated risk communication.

The study makes four contributions relevant to EID readers:

1. Exact reconciliation of 2019–2023 ECDC annual hantavirus totals across 142
EU/EEA country-years, providing a reproducible open baseline for future
surveillance evaluation.

2. Probabilistic evaluation using weighted interval score, 90% empirical
coverage, interval width, and mean absolute error — metrics that jointly assess
sharpness and calibration rather than point accuracy alone.

3. A transparent negative result: public demographic, land-use, and climate
covariates did not produce stable, calibrated improvement over simple
surveillance-history baselines. Covariate-augmented models had WIS more than
7 times worse than the best simple baseline in 2023.

4. A calibration–sharpness tradeoff figure that directly illustrates what public
annual country-level surveillance can and cannot support operationally.

The manuscript does not claim operational risk prediction, causal environmental
drivers, individual-level risk, or generalizability beyond EU/EEA annual
reported surveillance. All analyses are retrospective. No individual-level,
clinical, address-level, or restricted data were used; institutional review was
not required.
